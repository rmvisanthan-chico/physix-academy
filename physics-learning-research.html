Why active physics learning works

A plain-language summary of four well-cited studies — what they measured, what they found, and what they do not prove.

This page summarises published research. It is not a study of PhysiX Academy, and none of these results should be read as a promise about any individual student.

1. Students learn more when they interact with the physics

Hake (1998) surveyed mechanics test data from more than six thousand introductory physics students and compared courses using interactive engagement with traditional lecture-based courses. The interactive courses showed a much higher average normalised gain — roughly 0.48 against 0.23 for traditional instruction.

Hake, R.R. Interactive-engagement versus traditional methods. American Journal of Physics 66, 64–74 (1998). doi

2. Active learning improves exam performance across science

Freeman and colleagues (2014) ran a meta-analysis of 225 studies in undergraduate science, engineering and mathematics. Active learning raised examination performance by about 0.47 standard deviations on average, and failure rates fell from about 34% to about 22% compared with lecturing.

Freeman, S. et al. Active learning increases student performance in science, engineering, and mathematics. PNAS 111, 8410–8415 (2014). doi

3. Feeling of learning is not the same as learning

Deslauriers and colleagues (2019) found that students in active-learning classes learned more, but reported feeling that they had learned less than students in a polished lecture. Fluency is persuasive; effort is not. This is a useful warning against choosing a study method only because it feels smooth.

Deslauriers, L. et al. Measuring actual learning versus feeling of learning. PNAS 116, 19251–19257 (2019). doi

4. Simulations help when they are guided

Wieman, Adams and Perkins (2008) describe how well-designed simulations support conceptual understanding in physics — with an important condition: the simulation is embedded in a guided activity with a specific question to answer. An unguided sandbox is fun; a sandbox with a target is instruction.

Wieman, C.E., Adams, W.K., Perkins, K.K. PhET: Simulations that enhance learning. Science 322, 682–683 (2008). doi

What this means in practice

  • Change one variable at a time and predict the result before you look.
  • Write the relationship down before running the simulation, then check it.
  • Treat the simulation as a question to answer, not a video to watch.
  • If it feels easy and fluent, check whether you could reproduce the reasoning alone.

This is the design logic behind the simulations and the practice questions on PhysiX Academy: each simulation is paired with a chapter and a question, so the experiment has a purpose beyond itself.
